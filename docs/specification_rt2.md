# Railroad Tycoon II (1998)

## Scope and evidence

This specification describes the original 1998 release of *Railroad Tycoon II*. It focuses
on the player's decisions and the game feedback needed to make them: company finance,
track construction, stations, freight and passenger service, train operation, and scenario
objectives. It does not treat the later *Platinum* edition or *The Second Century*
expansion as part of the 1998 base game. The original user manual is the principal source
for controls and rules. Contemporary reviews provide independent context, while later
reference pages are used only for cross-checking basic release information. Source numbers
in brackets refer to the links at the end.

The manual documents a large set of scenario-specific rules and options. Accordingly, this
specification describes the shared systems and identifies where a scenario or selected
economic model changes their operation; it does not assume that every scenario uses every
feature. Where the available sources do not establish a detail, it is omitted rather than
inferred.

## Purpose and ways to play

The player builds and manages a railroad company in a historical setting. The central loop
is to raise capital, choose a route between places with economic activity, build track and
stations, assign trains and cargo cars, and earn revenue from transporting goods and
passengers. The player then uses company resources and personal investment to expand,
improve service, or compete with other railroads. Construction is not an end in itself:
the network must connect places that generate or demand cargo, and train service must
deliver it profitably. [1, chs. 1–3]

The base game offers a campaign, individual scenarios, and custom play. The campaign links
a sequence of 18 scenarios; individual scenarios present their own starting conditions and
objectives. Those objectives are not reducible to a single universal victory condition: a
scenario can require a particular financial result, network achievement, or other stated
target within its available period. The player selects a difficulty, and scenarios can set
their own parameters. A sandbox option removes financial and industrial constraints for
unconstrained construction and experimentation. [1, ch. 3; 12]

The manual describes three economic models—Basic, Advanced, and Expert—which control how
closely cargo and industry follow supply and demand. These are gameplay rules, not merely
difficulty labels. A scenario may specify the model, while custom play gives the player a
choice. Multiplayer supports competitive play, and the included map editor allows players
to create scenarios. [1, chs. 3, 10–11]

## The map, controls, and player feedback

The main play area is a rotatable, zoomable map. The player inspects terrain, towns,
industries, existing rail lines, and the locations available for development. Map overlays
help answer operational questions: resource views show where particular commodities are
available, while grade and traffic-density views help assess construction costs and
network use. These are useful because the same geographic connection can differ
substantially in cost or usefulness depending on terrain, cargo supply, and traffic. [1,
chs. 4–5]

The interface combines the map with lists and status information. The central list can
show stations, trains, players, and companies; selecting an entry brings the player to the
corresponding entity or its details. A message/activity area reports events and alerts.
The player can inspect a station's cargo, a train's assignment and condition, or a
company's financial state, then return to the map to act. Time controls let the player
pause or adjust the pace while making construction and operating decisions. [1, ch. 4]

Visual presentation conveys actionable state. Track geometry and terrain indicate the
route's physical demands; displayed grade and estimated cost inform a proposed
construction; station and train status communicate whether the network is connected and
operating. Color-coded stop indicators on a train's route show its loading policy. This
feedback supports decisions, but the manual's written rules remain authoritative:
decorative station architecture, for example, is distinct from station size and facilities
that affect operation. [1, chs. 4–6]

## Company formation and track construction

The player begins by establishing a company and choosing how much personal money to invest
alongside outside investment. The company is the vehicle for building and operating the
railroad; the player's personal financial position remains relevant through ownership and
share transactions. This separation makes a construction decision affect both the
company's liabilities and the player's exposure to its performance. [1, chs. 2, 7]

Track is laid by selecting a starting point and dragging toward an endpoint on the map.
The player chooses single or double track and, where a crossing is needed, a bridge type.
The interface displays information about the proposed alignment, including its grade and
cost, before construction is committed. Terrain and steepness affect cost, so a short
direct line is not automatically the best investment. The player can reroute around
difficult ground or pay to cross it, balancing up-front expense against the commercial
value of the connection. [1, ch. 5]

Stations are placed to serve towns, industries, and the surrounding area. A station need
not be connected at the moment it is placed, but it cannot function as part of the
railroad until linked to track. The player chooses among small, medium, and large
stations. Size determines the area that can be served and the station's ability to handle
traffic; building a larger station therefore trades greater reach and capacity for higher
cost. Stations can collect nearby resources and serve multiple businesses within their
coverage, so their placement determines which cargo sources and destinations a network can
reach. [1, ch. 5]

Track and station construction must also respect territorial access. The player may need
rights to build or operate in a region, and access to a rival's facilities can have a
cost. Competition is therefore partly geographical and partly financial: a company may
invest in its own route, negotiate or obtain access where available, or make use of
another railroad's infrastructure under the game's charges and revenue-sharing rules. [1,
chs. 5, 8]

## Stations, cargo, and the economic models

Cargo originates at and is demanded by towns, farms, mines, factories, and other
industries. A station's catchment area determines which of those locations can use it.
Connecting a producer to a consumer creates a transport opportunity, but the commodity's
availability, value, and industrial requirements depend on the selected economic model.
Freight revenue is affected by the cargo type, distance, speed of delivery, and the demand
at the destination. A network is valuable when its services match those opportunities, not
simply when it has many miles of track. [1, chs. 5–6]

The Basic model simplifies the economy. Industries produce without requiring the same
degree of input supply, and stations accept cargo more broadly, with prices adjusted to
reflect that simplified acceptance. It is consequently easier to establish service without
first constructing a complete chain of supplying and consuming industries.

The Advanced model makes production more dependent on required inputs. A factory that
needs raw materials must receive them for its production chain to function. Stations still
accept cargo beyond what a particular industry can use, but at reduced rates. The player
must pay more attention to what each location produces and consumes and to whether the
railroad can deliver the necessary inputs.

The Expert model adds further market and investment decisions. The player can purchase
industries, and demand levels and prices vary. Because a business can require supply and
its commercial value can change with demand, the player must consider both transport
routes and the industries that create or consume the cargo. The manual also describes
advanced financial options associated with Expert play. The exact model available in a
scenario depends on its settings. [1, chs. 3, 6–7]

These models change the planning problem. In Basic, broad acceptance makes a first network
more forgiving. In Advanced and Expert, the player needs to identify a working chain—for
example, moving a raw material to an industry that uses it and distributing the resulting
goods to a market. The game presents cargo and industry information through station and
map views so that the player can judge whether an intended service has a source, a
destination, and a plausible return. No model removes the need to pay for infrastructure
and train operation.

Stations can be improved with facilities that affect how trains are handled and
maintained. Such upgrades can reduce operating delays or improve station service, but they
require investment and are useful only where traffic justifies the expense. Station
buildings and architectural appearance do not themselves replace the functional choices of
size, location, connection, and improvement. [1, ch. 5]

## Trains, routes, and service

The player selects locomotives and assigns trains to routes. Locomotives differ in
purchase cost, maintenance, fuel use, acceleration, reliability, and performance at speed,
under load, and on grades. These characteristics make engine selection a route decision: a
powerful locomotive may be appropriate for heavy traffic or steep track, while its cost
and upkeep may not be justified on a lightly used line. The manual provides locomotive
statistics for comparison rather than presenting one universally best engine. [1, ch. 6]

A train's route is built from station stops, and the player assigns a consist—the cars
carried—to the train. The consist determines the kinds of cargo the train can collect and
deliver. At a stop, the player can specify whether the train departs immediately, waits
until at least half its cars are full, or waits until all are full. The stop's colored
indicator communicates that setting. Waiting can improve a train's load, but also delays
delivery and ties up the train; an immediate departure can preserve schedule but carry
less cargo. The player configures each route to balance these costs. [1, ch. 6]

Trains can be assigned operating classes such as Express, Normal, Slow, or Halted. Classes
help manage how services use the network and whether a train is actively running. A line
with multiple services may need different treatment for fast passenger traffic and slower
freight, and the player's choices affect congestion and punctuality. The map's
traffic-density view helps identify heavily used sections that may merit additional track
or revised service. [1, chs. 4, 6]

Operation has continuing resource and maintenance demands. Trains consume fuel; steam
locomotives also require water, and sand is part of their operation. Engines age, require
maintenance, and may break down. Throttle and condition influence running speed and costs,
so the player must balance fast delivery against wear and expense. Station improvements
and the placement of facilities support this maintenance. The player can also choose cars
such as dining cars, which increase passenger revenue, or cabooses, which the manual
associates with safety and security benefits. [1, ch. 6]

Passenger and mail service complements freight. Passenger revenue is tied to service and
the value of the journey, and faster delivery can improve the return. Dining cars can
increase passenger income. Cargo, mail, and passengers thus compete for train capacity and
priority: the player chooses consists and service patterns according to what the route can
earn and what the scenario requires. [1, ch. 6]

The game also permits use of rival infrastructure under specified charges. When a train
travels over a competitor's track, revenue is shared according to the distance travelled
on that track; use of a rival station can add per-car charges. These rules make an
existing line a possible shortcut, but not free capacity. The player must compare the cost
of access with the construction and operating cost of a separate route. [1, chs. 5, 8]

## Finance, ownership, and competition

The company must finance track, stations, locomotives, and improvements while meeting
ongoing costs. Its financial position is distinct from the player's personal wealth. The
player can raise money through company share issues and bonds, and can manage ownership
through share purchases and buybacks. Dividends and the changing value of shares connect
operating results to the player's personal investment. The interface provides company and
stock information for evaluating these choices. [1, ch. 7]

In the more advanced financial rules, the player can also use margin buying and short
selling. These are additional ways to take financial positions in a company's shares and
carry greater exposure than simply holding an investment. The manual presents these as
Expert-level options; they should not be assumed to be active in every scenario. [1, ch.
7]

Other companies compete for routes, traffic, and market position. The player can buy
shares in rivals, and corporate transactions can include mergers or takeovers. Such
decisions use capital that could otherwise expand the railroad. Bankruptcy is a possible
financial outcome, not merely a loss of track: a company that cannot meet its obligations
may fail. Scenario goals and rules determine how these systems contribute to success, so
the player must assess both the operating network and its balance sheet. [1, chs. 7–8]

Goodwill and territorial access are also part of competition. A company may need rights to
operate in an area, while sharing or purchasing access to another railroad's track or
stations can extend service without building a parallel line. Those arrangements create
expenses or revenue shares, so they are alternatives with explicit costs rather than
unrestricted access. [1, chs. 5, 8]

## Scenarios, multiplayer, and the editor

Scenarios provide the context for the mechanics: a map, a starting position, economic and
financial conditions, and objectives. The player decides how to pursue the stated goal
within the scenario's constraints. Campaign play carries the player through a sequence of
scenarios; standalone play allows an individual scenario to be selected. A scenario may
limit the available time, set an economic model, or define particular requirements, so its
objective text and initial conditions are essential parts of the rules. [1, ch. 3]

Multiplayer makes the same network and company decisions competitive between human
players. The manual describes network play and direct connection methods, including
TCP/IP, IPX, modem, and serial connections. Players compete within the chosen scenario
rather than receiving a separate set of railroad-building mechanics. Their ability to
access rival infrastructure and the financial market makes the interaction relevant to
both route planning and company strategy. [1, ch. 10]

The map editor lets players create and configure scenarios. Its purpose is to define the
setting in which companies operate, including terrain, industries, and scenario
conditions. It extends replayability, but is not required for the standard campaign or
individual scenarios. [1, ch. 11]
